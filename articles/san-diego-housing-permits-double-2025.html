San Diego Nearly Doubles Housing Permit Approvals: What the Numbers Really Mean

February 4, 2026·Robert Amarin, Owner & General Contractor·5 min read

San Diego's housing permit numbers looked dramatically better in 2025. The city approved roughly 14,500 permits, nearly twice the prior year's total. Mayor Todd Gloria pointed to that figure as evidence of meaningful progress on housing supply. From where we sit as builders on the ground, though, the headline number only tells part of the story.

Understanding the permit surge

The jump in approved permits reflects real effort from the city to move applications through the system faster. Streamlined review processes, state pressure through the Regional Housing Needs Allocation (RHNA) framework, and a concerted push from Mayor Gloria's office all contributed to higher approval volumes in 2025.

San Diego is required under state law to plan for and accommodate significant housing production in each planning cycle. The RHNA targets the city is working toward call for roughly 28,000 new homes, a number that makes 14,500 permits look more like a step in the right direction than a destination.

What a permit approval actually represents

A permit approval is not a completed home. It is authorization to build, and between the approval and move-in, a project still has to clear construction financing, secure a contractor, work through inspections, and navigate whatever site-specific complications arise along the way.

In a high-cost market like San Diego, that gap between permit and completion is where a substantial number of projects stall or get delayed. Labor availability, material costs, and the complexity of individual sites all affect how quickly an approved project actually gets built.

This is especially true for ADUs. While state law requires cities to act on complete ADU applications within 60 days, the full arc from initial submittal to a construction-ready approval, including plan revisions, corrections, and departmental coordination, commonly runs six to twelve months depending on the jurisdiction and project complexity. That is the real timeline homeowners need to plan around.

Why builders say progress isn't enough

Local developers and contractors have been consistent about the challenges that do not show up in permit statistics. Construction costs in San Diego remain among the highest in the state. Skilled labor in trades like framing, concrete, and electrical is competitive and sometimes hard to schedule quickly. And permitting improvements, while real, have been uneven across different project types and jurisdictions.

For smaller builders and individual homeowners, the people most likely to be building ADUs and smaller infill projects, those barriers are felt most acutely. A large developer can absorb delays and carry costs across a portfolio of projects. A homeowner building a single ADU is working on a tighter margin, where every month of delay has a direct financial consequence.

The affordability picture behind the numbers

The reason San Diego's permit totals get so much attention is that housing affordability in the city remains a serious and persistent challenge. Median home prices are well above national averages, and rental costs have not declined meaningfully despite elevated mortgage rates that have slowed buying activity in many markets.

More permits help, but supply additions take time to move through construction and reach occupancy. Even an optimistic scenario in which every permit issued in 2025 translates to a completed home within two years would not close the gap between current supply and the city's housing needs.

What it means if you're planning to build

For homeowners considering an ADU or other residential project, the broader supply environment actually works in your favor in one important way: the political and regulatory climate is oriented toward getting housing built. State laws protecting ADU applicants from excessive local delays, streamlined plan check processes, and reduced barriers to construction all reflect an environment where adding a unit to your property is more achievable than it was five years ago.

The practical implication is that submitting a complete, well-prepared permit application today is more likely to move through the system efficiently than it would have been in earlier years. The city has invested in process improvements, and experienced contractors who know what reviewers want to see can help you take advantage of that.

Quick Tip

Permit approval and project completion are two different milestones, often separated by months of construction work, inspections, and final sign-offs. When budgeting and planning an ADU or residential addition, build your timeline around construction completion, not permit issuance. That means accounting for contractor scheduling, material lead times, and the inspection process before you project a move-in or rental-ready date.
